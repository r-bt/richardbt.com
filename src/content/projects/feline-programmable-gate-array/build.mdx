## Audio input

We used [SPH0645LM4H-B](https://www.adafruit.com/product/3421) I2S MEMS microphones on Adafruit breakout boards.

To localize something from sound we needed four microphones: three arranged in a triangle around one central microphone. The idea is that there will be a time delay between when the sound reaches each microphone (encoded in the phase of the audio signal). Based on this time delay you can work out the location. We placed the microphones on a 3 cm grid: if the mics were further apart than half a wavelength, the timing would become ambiguous.

The microphones speak a slightly non-standard version of the usual I<sup>2</sup>S protocol, so I wrote my own receiver.

The audio was initially very quiet from these microphones. I noticed the six most significant bits they sent were always 1. Left-shifting the audio by six bits made it much louder and easier to work with.

### Filtering

Human speech typically sits between 20 Hz and 3 kHz, but we sampled at 32 kHz, so I filtered out everything above speech and kept only every eighth sample, bringing the sample rate down to 4 kHz.

## Sound localization

The phase difference between microphones tells you the direction of the sound source.

An FFT splits the sound into its individual frequencies, and each one gives its own estimate of direction. I kept the frequencies in the range of human speech, roughly 100 Hz to 2.65 kHz (216 of them). For each frequency I:

1. Calculated the phase difference between the central microphone and each of the others
2. Scaled each peripheral microphone's position by its phase difference
3. Summed the scaled positions to get a direction
4. Assigned the direction to one of 16 bins, adding its strength to that bin

## Motor control

To test localization, I generated PWM signals to drive a servo, mapping each bin to a different duty cycle so the servo pointed at that angle. Once the servo could consistently point towards me when I spoke I was confident the localization was working.

### DC motor control

Initially I tried using continuous servos to move the robot, but they made enough noise to interfere with localization. Instead we used two Adafruit DC Gearbox TT Motors. These have three inputs: IN1, IN2 and EN. IN1 and IN2 set the direction, and since the motors only ever turned clockwise, they were always low and high respectively. EN enables the motor, so I drove it with a PWM signal to vary the speed. I chose 20 kHz so it wouldn't interfere with localization or voice biometrics.

### Steering

The robot only moves if it recognizes its owner's voice. Then it steers by the winning bin: bins 6–11 (on its left) turn on only the right motor, bins 0–2 and 12–15 (on its right) only the left, and bins 3–5 (in front) both.

## Results

When everything came together, the robot recognized its owner's voice, turned towards them and drove over. With someone else talking it moved much less, often not at all.

It wasn't perfect. Voice recognition gave false positives for people with a similar pitch to the owner. Localization also struggled with pauses in speech, until we added a volume threshold so the robot ignores quiet moments and keeps heading the way it was going.
